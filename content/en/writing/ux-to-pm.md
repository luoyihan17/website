Recently, I have been circling around one question: **are interaction designers and product managers really as far apart as we often imagine?**

I started thinking seriously about this because my work has changed a lot over the past few years. When I first entered AI music projects, I was still a very typical interaction designer. I cared about how users entered the creation flow, what they should input first, how to handle long generation times, how someone with no music creation experience could understand what AI could help them do, and how previewing, editing, and payment should connect.

At that time, I naturally understood the problem as: **how should this feature be designed?** But as I went deeper into the project, I found that the questions I asked slowly changed.

## From "How Do We Design It?" to "What Should We Build?"

In the earliest AI song creation work, much of the product logic was still organized around traditional music creation: users entered lyrics, selected a style, and generated a song. From an interaction perspective, this flow could certainly be optimized again and again. The entry point could be simpler, the filling cost could be lower, and the generation process could be clearer.

But later I began to feel that the real problem might not be the flow. It was: **do ordinary users really think they are "composing"?**

Most people may not know how to write lyrics at all. They may not know what genre, BPM, or arrangement means. They may simply have a sentence they suddenly want to say, a story, a photo, a feeling, or just a melody they hummed casually.

If what users truly want is to turn their own expression into a song, why must the product require them to begin in the way professional music creation begins? Starting from this thinking, we later tried different directions such as conversational song creation, image-to-song, one-sentence song creation, and humming-to-song.

This stage had a strong impact on me because it was the first time I clearly realized: **I was no longer only optimizing an already defined requirement. I was participating in deciding the requirement itself.** "How should this page be made?" and "Does the user actually need this page?" are two completely different kinds of work. The former searches for an answer. The latter sometimes requires doubting the question first.

## After Launch, I Started Understanding What "Results" Really Mean

Another change was that I began to care more and more about what happened after launch. In the past, as a designer, I could easily treat "the design goes live" as a stage endpoint. But after working on commercial products, I increasingly felt that launch is the real beginning.

Did users actually generate anything? At which step did they leave? Why did they not return after the first generation? Why were some people willing to generate many times in a row? Why did users not pay after previewing? Did the experience we thought was smooth really make users feel it was easier? All of these questions eventually become data.

In AI Singing, we reworked the preview and payment path. In AI song creation, we also continuously shortened the creation path and adjusted entry points and generation methods. When business data later truly changed, I gained a new understanding of "design results."

It was not because I started thinking data was more important than experience. It was because I began to realize: **design files are not the result.** Passing review is not the result. Development completion is not the result. The real result is what actually happens after it reaches users' hands.

From that point on, the boundary of my responsibility slowly changed. Before, I would think: "Whether this feature experience is smooth is what I need to be responsible for." Later, I would continue asking whether anyone was using it, why they were using it, whether there was conversion, whether it was worth continuing to invest in, and if performance was poor, whether the problem was the experience or whether the thing we chose to build was invalid from the beginning.

## Vemus: Thinking from the Perspective of a Whole Product

If AI song creation brought me into product definition, then Vemus was the first time I very clearly thought from the perspective of a complete product. Making an independent app is completely different from making a feature inside QQ Music.

Mature platforms already have many default answers. Why users open QQ Music, how accounts log in, how content is distributed, and how payment is completed: this infrastructure already exists. But for an independent product from 0 to 1, there are not that many ready-made answers.

When working on Vemus, the question I began to face became: **why do we still need an independent AI music app?** If QQ Music already has AI song creation, why would users need to download Vemus? Should it serve the same group of people? Do users really need "generating one song once," or a place where they can continuously create music? Why would someone come back the next day after the first generation?

I also had to ask which part should be the most core in the first phase of the product: generation, consumption, community, identity, or content. Which features must be built first? Which features look good but would only slow down the launch if built now?

By this point, it was already difficult for me to separate the work very clearly into "product" and "design." In the end, everything connects. Product positioning determines what the home page looks like. Model capability determines user expectations. Commercialization affects the generation path. Engineering resources determine feature priority.

A plan that feels complete in experience does not mean it is worth investing in right now. And a feature that is not yet 100 percent complete may still deserve to launch first if it can most quickly validate the most important product assumption.

I used to habitually ask: **how can we make this better?** After Vemus, I began asking more often: **is this actually worth doing right now?** I think this was a turning point when I truly began moving from designer toward product manager.

## Mini-games: From Receiving Requirements to Creating the Project

What truly made me certain that I like doing product work was a series of music mini-games I initiated later. These projects were very different from most requirements I had received before because **they did not even exist at the beginning.**

No one gave me a PRD saying, "We now need a music mini-game. Please take charge of the design." At the beginning, it was more that I saw an opportunity myself.

Every time a music platform encounters a new song release, artist collaboration, or fan operation, there are many campaign needs. But many traditional campaign formats are still pages, charts, check-ins, and lotteries. At that time, I was thinking: **why can't music be played?**

A song clearly has rhythm, lyrics, visuals, a world view, and artist content. Why can these things only be displayed, instead of directly becoming an interaction mechanism? So I began actively proposing the direction of mini-games.

From this point on, what I did was already very different from an ordinary interaction design requirement. I needed to find the right project opportunity, judge what kind of gameplay fit each song, propose the product concept myself, evaluate whether the idea could actually land, find development resources, coordinate business, design, artist assets, and engineering, control scope, and hit operations and release timelines.

Sometimes, when development resources were insufficient, I continued from design into frontend implementation and deployment myself. Finally, the project launched, and then I watched whether real users actually played it.

## From One Song to a Small Product That Actually Launched

Take aespa's "Lemonade" project as an example. At the beginning, no one told me, "Let's make an ordering game." I first looked for parts of the song and artist content that were suitable for interaction, and then gradually transformed the Lemonade concept into a scene that could actually be played.

In this process, what I considered was no longer only whether the interface looked good or whether the buttons felt smooth. I had to ask: why this gameplay? Does it actually relate to the song? Can users understand it at first glance? Can they get feedback within one minute? Why would they be willing to play again? What level of complexity can our current development resources support? To meet the launch timeline, what content must be kept, and what must be cut?

In the end, this project had more than 30,000 participants within four days after launch. Later, Lay Zhang's side-scrolling action project followed a similar process and gained more than 30,000 participants in three days.

Looking back at these projects now, the most important thing is no longer just "I designed a few mini-games." It is that **these were projects I actively initiated and pushed all the way from idea to launch.**

At the beginning, there was no requirement, no complete team, and not even a confirmed gameplay direction. I had to first prove that this direction was worth trying, then find resources to make it happen. From opportunity identification, to product concept, to gameplay definition, to resource coordination, to engineering delivery, to final launch validation, this entire chain is actually very close to what a 0-to-1 product manager does.

I only realized later that I really liked this state of work. I do not only like solving an already existing problem well. What I like more is **discovering an opportunity that did not originally exist, and then finding a way to make it happen.**

## From One Successful Case to a Repeatable Capability

After doing this several times, my focus changed again. At first I was thinking: **can this song be turned into a game?** Later I began thinking: **can mini-games become a long-term capability of music products?**

If an artist's new song can be turned into a mini-game, what can be reused? Can gameplay mechanisms be standardized? Can a song's BPM, sections, lyrics, and emotions directly enter game mechanics? What types of gameplay are better for acquiring new users, easier to share, or better for fan retention?

I also started asking whether mini-games had to exist only inside one-off H5 pages. Could they enter the player? Could they enter digital albums? Could they be combined with artist operations, membership benefits, or even offline experiences?

When I began asking these questions, I was no longer only designing one specific game. I began thinking: **can a creative idea that succeeded by chance be turned into a product capability that can happen repeatedly?**

This change was important to me. As a designer, I used to easily focus on how to make this one experience as good as possible. But now I ask one step further: why did it work this time? Which parts can be copied? Will it still work next time? If we do it ten times a year, can the current method still run? Is this direction worth continuous investment?

## What Is the Difference Between Designers and Product Managers?

If you ask me now whether interaction designers and product managers should merge, I do not have an especially absolute answer. I do not think all designers should become product managers. I also do not think the two roles will eventually become exactly the same. In mature large products, professional specialization is still very important.

But I do increasingly feel that, especially in AI products, innovative businesses, and 0-to-1 projects, the boundary between design and product will become more and more blurred. Many times, we do not even know what the right product form is yet. Is AI a tool? A creative partner? A content producer? Or a new way of consumption?

These answers are hard to define completely from the beginning. Many times, you have to really build it, let users use it, and then come back and revise your judgment. The prototype itself is product discussion. An interaction method can sometimes directly change the requirement. A single piece of user feedback can also overturn the product's initial assumption.

At least in my own work, there is rarely a very clean process where **product defines the requirement clearly, designer executes, and developer implements.** More often, product and experience are made together.

## Why I Believe I Can Become a Product Manager

Now, I actually do not really want to say that I am moving from design to product, because I do not feel that I have left design. Many of the judgments I make when doing product work still come from design training.

When discussing a feature, I still naturally ask whether the user really knows this thing exists, why they would tap it, whether they can understand it the first time they use it, and whether a flow that makes sense logically actually feels natural. These habits will not disappear because a title changes.

But now, I continue thinking further. Why build this feature? Is it the most important thing right now? How many resources is it worth investing? How should we coordinate people to make it happen? When should it launch? What should we use after launch to judge whether it is valid? If there is no result, should we keep optimizing it, or should we give it up?

I think this is my biggest change over the past few years. Before, I hoped I could be responsible for a good experience. Now, I hope I can also be responsible for product direction, priority, resources, delivery, growth, business results, and even whether a project should continue or stop.

If I had to describe the biggest difference between designers and product managers, I would now prefer to understand it as: **how large a result are you willing to be responsible for?**

I still care about a button, a path, a piece of feedback, and whether an animation feels natural enough. But now I also want to know why this button exists, why this feature is worth building, why this product is needed by users, why build it now instead of something else, and after spending so many people and so much time to build it, **did it actually produce a result?**

So for me, moving from interaction design toward product is not leaving design. It is simply that I have started hoping I can be **responsible for a larger result.**
