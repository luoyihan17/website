## Curatorial Profile

I am a curator with a background in interaction design, game design, and new media art, with long-term attention to the changing relationships among art, design, technology, and society. My curatorial practice spans international young artists, artificial intelligence, robotics, interactive installations, future lifestyles, and interdisciplinary design. I am committed to building connections among creators, technology, space, and the public through exhibitions. Compared with curatorial paths centered on traditional art historical research and artwork display, my approach is more grounded in interaction design, user experience, and systems thinking. I care not only about what a work expresses, but also about how audiences enter the exhibition, how they understand works, how they move through space, and how they build real emotional connections with abstract issues in art and technology.

For me, curation is not simply placing a group of works in the same space. It is building a narrative system made of themes, works, space, media, visitor flow, and public communication. A curator needs to discover relationships among many works and pieces of information from different backgrounds, build a clear content structure, and translate complex issues into cultural experiences the public can view, understand, participate in, and discuss.

My curatorial practice began with attention to exchange and exhibition opportunities for international young creators.

In 2021, against the backdrop of global pandemic restrictions that limited international exchange and offline exhibitions, I co-initiated **OMNI Art Expo** with student organizations from art schools in China and abroad. The project connected student associations from 10 art and design colleges across the United States, opened a call for works from interdisciplinary young creators, received more than 1,000 submissions, and selected around 200 works for a centralized exhibition at Xintai Warehouse in Shanghai.

**OMNI Art Expo** was not only an exhibition of young artists' works, but also a cross-school, cross-cultural, and interdisciplinary curatorial experiment. The participating works covered visual art, product design, fashion design, architecture, interaction design, new media art, and experimental creation. My work included project initiation, curatorial theme and content direction, school and creator resource coordination, open call management, content selection, exhibition structure planning, spatial display design, visual material design, and on-site delivery coordination.

The exhibition venue, Shanghai Xintai Warehouse, is a century-old industrial building that was formerly a textile factory. Facing the space's exposed brick walls, industrial structure, and historical traces, we did not treat the venue as a completely neutral white box. Instead, we regarded the building itself as part of the exhibition narrative.

In the exhibition design, we continued the original brick texture of the architecture and used stacked bricks in the main hall to form display structures, allowing contemporary design in the exhibition to enter into direct dialogue with industrial heritage. For example, by placing Parsons fashion design works and Cornell-related design drawings within the same spatial relationship, the juxtaposition of different disciplines, materials, and creative methods presented a collision between traditional industrial history and future design language.

OMNI Art Expo was the first time I fully participated in and pushed a large cross-school art project from concept formation to public exhibition. It required not only curatorial judgment and content organization, but also coordination among schools, creators, venues, construction, communication, and public resources. The project ultimately received support and visits from the Shanghai municipal government, which also made me realize that curation can become an important medium connecting scattered creative resources, urban cultural spaces, and public society.

After OMNI Art Expo, my curatorial research gradually extended from international young art and interdisciplinary design toward technology art, robotics, and future society.

In 2025, I participated as a curator in planning the **2025 International Robotics and Art Design Biennale**. The project was held from August 2 to September 30, 2025, at Robot World in Beijing E-Town, and served as a companion art and design exhibition for the 2025 World Robot Conference.

The exhibition centered on the theme "Future Symbionts: Robots, Art, and Humanity," discussing how robots are gradually moving from tools of industrial production and technical efficiency into culture, emotion, home life, artistic creation, and social relationships. The project invited submissions from leading domestic art schools, overseas art and design colleges, young artists, robot designers, and interdisciplinary research teams. It received more than 200 works and selected more than 40 for exhibition.

In this project, I participated in and pushed forward work including curatorial theme definition, exhibition content framework, international open calls, work selection and review, artist and school communication, exhibition zone structure, spatial content organization, visitor flow, visual materials, venue construction, simple renovation, and on-site presentation.

Facing different types of content, including robot products, artificial intelligence art, generative design, intelligent interactive installations, bionic machinery, virtual reality, future home design, and international design award works, I needed to establish a unified but open curatorial logic. This allowed works from art, design, technology, and industry to form a dialogue within the same exhibition instead of becoming isolated technical displays.

In work selection, I focused not only on visual effect and technical completion, but also on conceptual integrity, innovation, social value, on-site presentation conditions, and public communication ability. For works using artificial intelligence and robotics, I paid particular attention to whether the technology truly participated in the work's expression, or was only used as formal packaging.

The exhibition did not treat robots simply as technological products to be displayed. Instead, it regarded them as new subjects entering human daily life and social relationships. Through combinations of different works, the exhibition attempted to raise a series of questions: when robots begin to have human-like bodies, language, and emotional expression, how will humans re-understand intelligence, labor, companionship, and creation? When artificial intelligence begins to participate in art production, how will the relationship among creators, tools, and works change?

To lower the comprehension barrier between frontier technology and ordinary audiences, I considered content research, visual experience, and public participation at the same time within the exhibition structure. Through work sections, spatial flow, and narrative rhythm, I guided audiences from intuitive recognition of robot forms and functions toward deeper discussion of human-machine relationships, emotional projection, and future society.

From OMNI Art Expo to the 2025 International Robotics and Art Design Biennale, my curatorial practice gradually formed a clear path: from connecting international young art and design resources, to exploring interdisciplinary display in historical architecture, and then to organizing public discussions around robotics, artificial intelligence, and technology art.

These two curatorial practices also correspond to changes in how I understand the curator's role.

In OMNI Art Expo, I was more concerned with building a platform for exhibition and exchange among young creators across schools. In the International Robotics and Art Design Biennale, I further focused on how curation can respond to ongoing technological change, and how art, design, industry, and the public can enter dialogue around future issues.

My interaction design background helps me understand audience behavior and information reception in space. My game design experience makes me pay attention to participation rules, experience rhythm, and feedback mechanisms. New media and technical practice also allow me to understand the implementation logic behind artworks and communicate effectively with artists, designers, engineers, and technical teams.

Together, these experiences formed my curatorial method:

**Build viewpoints through research, organize content through narrative, shape experience through design, and push exhibitions into reality through interdisciplinary collaboration.**

I believe curation is not only the selection and display of existing works, but also an active form of knowledge production. Curators need to discover connections among different disciplines, cultures, and creative methods, raise questions with public value through exhibitions, and create a discussion space where different viewpoints can appear together.

I hope to use curation to connect art and technology, history and future, creators and the public, academic research and industry practice. For technology art exhibitions, I care not only about "what technology can do," but also want to further ask: "Why do we need it?", "How is it changing us?", and "What kind of future do we want to move toward together with technology?"

My goal is not to add artistic decoration to technology, nor to turn art into an explanation of technical achievements. It is to let art, design, and technology intervene in and question one another, and jointly participate in imagining and constructing future society.
